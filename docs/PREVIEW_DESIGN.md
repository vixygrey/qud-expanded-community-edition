# Stratigraphic Terminal

**A design philosophy for depth rendered on a grid.**

---

## The movement

Every surface is a cross-section. What appears to be a plane is always an edge — the exposed face
of something accumulated, layer upon layer, over a duration no single viewer can hold. This
philosophy takes that cut face as its only subject. It does not illustrate depth; it *exhibits* it,
the way a core sample exhibits ten thousand years by simply being lifted into the light and laid
flat. The work is an instrument reading, not a picture. It is meticulously crafted to look measured
rather than composed — as though the hand that made it were calibrating something, and beauty were
a side effect of precision.

## Space and form

Horizontal strata govern everything. Bands stack, thicken, thin, and interrupt one another with the
patience of sediment, and the eye reads them from top to bottom as time reads downward. Against this
lateral insistence, one vertical incident — a single column, seam, or intrusion — pierces the whole
stack and organises it. That intrusion is the composition's spine and its only violence. Nothing
else is permitted to be vertical. The tension between the many horizontals and the one vertical is
the entire structural argument, and it must be resolved with the painstaking attention of a master
draughtsman: every band edge deliberate, every interval measured, nothing arbitrary.

## The grid beneath

All of it sits on a discrete lattice — a fixed cell, rectangular and slightly taller than it is
wide, from which no mark may escape. Marks land on the lattice or they do not land at all. This
constraint is not a limitation but a discipline, and the finished work should betray countless hours
spent negotiating with it: forms that appear organic while remaining perfectly quantised, curves
that are secretly staircases, gradients that are secretly counted. The lattice should be sensed
rather than seen — a faint order under everything, occasionally surfacing where a band edge steps
instead of slicing.

## Colour and material

The palette is fixed and small, and this is the point. A restricted set forces meaning into
*adjacency* rather than into hue: two bands differ, and the difference carries the information. A
deep desaturated ground dominates — the colour of a dark room, of unlit depth — and against it the
warm register (gold, oxide, rust) reads as the surface world, the lit and living stratum. Cool
accents mark the anomalous. Brightest value is spent once, and only once, at the point of greatest
consequence. Colour is never decorative; it is a legend.

## Light and decay

The image is seen *through* something — glass, distance, an old apparatus. A vignette closes the
corners, drawing the field inward and admitting that the view is instrumented rather than direct.
Fine horizontal scan lines lie across the whole surface at a regular interval, so consistently that
they read as material rather than effect, like the tooth of a paper. Both must be executed with
restraint bordering on invisibility — present at full size, unnoticed at a glance, discovered on the
second look. This is where the craftsmanship lives, and where amateur work always fails: the
temptation is to make the artefact loud. Resist it entirely.

## Typography and legibility

Text is a specimen label, not a title. Monospaced, letterspaced wide, small, and placed on the
lattice like everything else — it belongs to the instrument, not to the artist. One phrase may carry
weight; everything else whispers at the margin as annotation, index, or coordinate. The work must
survive brutal reduction: at a quarter of its size the strata, the intrusion, and one line of text
must still resolve cleanly, and everything that cannot survive that reduction should be built to
disappear gracefully rather than to smear. Design for the small view first and let the large view be
the reward for looking closer — a piece that holds at both scales is the product of deep expertise,
and holding at only one is the mark of a draft.

## The standard

The finished object should read as an artefact from a discipline that does not exist: a plate from
some patient survey, catalogued and filed. Nothing overlaps. Nothing crowds an edge. Every interval
is intentional and every alignment survives measurement. The whole must look laboured over —
the result of someone at the top of their field making a hundred small decisions correctly, then
removing every element that could not justify itself.
